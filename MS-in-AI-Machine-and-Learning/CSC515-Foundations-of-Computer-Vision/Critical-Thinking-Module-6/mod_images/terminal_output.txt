CSC515 Module 6 - Adaptive Thresholding Under Changing Illumination

========================================================================================
STEP 1/5: Prepare the two full scenes
========================================================================================

This assignment examines two mugs under different lighting conditions. One mug is glossy
black, and the other is light-colored with raised flowers. Both sit on a light tabletop
in front of a dark background. The first photograph is lit from the white mug's side. In
the second, the light comes from the black mug's side. The goal is to see whether the
same adaptive thresholding settings still work after that change.

Light from the white mug's side: original 2134 x 1765 pixels; processed 1000 x 827
pixels.

Light from the black mug's side: original 2222 x 1543 pixels; processed 1000 x 694
pixels.

In load_and_prepare(), cv2.resize() reduces both photographs to 1,000 pixels wide while
keeping their original proportions. Neither image is cropped, so the mugs, tabletop,
background, and shadows all remain. The first view shows the original photographs beside
their grayscale versions.

OpenCV reads color in BGR order, meaning blue, green, and red. Then cv2.cvtColor() gives
each pixel one brightness value instead of three color values. The grayscale image uses
uint8 values from 0, black, to 255, white. A 5 x 5 Gaussian blur reduces small
brightness variations before thresholding. With sigmaX=0, OpenCV calculates sigma from
the kernel size. Both adaptive calculations use this same blurred grayscale input for
each photograph.

The blur does not remove the larger shadows or the reflections on the black mug. Look at
its bright patches and the flowers on the white mug. These are details in the scene, and
they also change the brightness values. Treating all of them as random noise would miss
part of the problem.

This run uses Python 3.12.13; OpenCV 4.13.0; NumPy 2.5.3; Matplotlib 3.11.2.

========================================================================================
STEP 2/5: Threshold the scene lit from the white mug's side
========================================================================================

Start with the photograph lit from the white mug's side. The left panel shows the
blurred grayscale input, and the right panel shows the initial adaptive image. Look at
the rims, handles, and raised flowers to see which details remain visible.

Adaptive thresholding calculates a separate cutoff for each pixel using the brightness
of its neighbors. Here, ADAPTIVE_THRESH_GAUSSIAN_C calculates a weighted local average
and subtracts C. The initial settings are blockSize=31 and C=5, so the program examines
a 31 x 31 window and subtracts 5 from that average. THRESH_BINARY_INV makes pixels at or
below their threshold white (255), while brighter pixels become black (0).

A white pixel does not necessarily belong to a mug. It only means that the pixel met its
local threshold. For example, a shadow on the tabletop can be selected along with a dark
part of a handle. The table counts those selected pixels and their connected regions.

    Mask                  White pixels    Image %    Regions
    initial_adaptive            66,762     8.0728        380

Some of the mug boundaries are visible, although several areas remain thin or
disconnected. The black mug's bright reflections interrupt parts of its outline. The
white mug has a different problem: parts of its boundary are close in brightness to the
tabletop.

========================================================================================
STEP 3/5: Use the same scheme with light from the black mug's side
========================================================================================

Now look at the photograph lit from the black mug's side. The program uses the same
preparation and initial thresholding settings as in Step 2. Compare the right panel with
the previous result, particularly around the black mug's reflections and the tabletop
shadows.

The settings remain ADAPTIVE_THRESH_GAUSSIAN_C, THRESH_BINARY_INV, blockSize=31, and
C=5. Each pixel is still compared with its weighted local average minus 5. Values at or
below that cutoff become white (255); brighter values become black (0).

The black mug can still contain bright reflections, while the tabletop contains dark
shadows. This is why changing the light does not make every selected region part of a
mug. The table describes the pixels selected in this second photograph.

    Mask                  White pixels    Image %    Regions
    initial_adaptive            66,728     9.6150        301

Changing the light affects which parts of the mugs appear in the binary image. The same
settings still pick up some boundaries, but they do not separate both mugs completely
from their surroundings. The next step revises the settings and checks both photographs
again.

========================================================================================
STEP 4/5: Revise the adaptive settings for both photographs
========================================================================================

The initial results leave several boundaries thin or disconnected. For the revision, the
window increases to 101 x 101 and C changes to 10. Both photographs use these new
settings. The program starts again from each blurred grayscale image, so the revised
result is calculated independently of the initial binary image.

The larger window includes a wider area when calculating the local average. Increasing C
lowers the threshold, which selects fewer white pixels if that average stays the same.
However, the window also changes here, so its average can change too. This comparison
shows their combined effect; it cannot tell us how much each setting contributed.

Light from the white mug's side.
    Mask                  White pixels    Image %    Regions
    initial_adaptive            66,762     8.0728        380
    revised_adaptive            96,910    11.7183        150

Light from the black mug's side.
    Mask                  White pixels    Image %    Regions
    initial_adaptive            66,728     9.6150        301
    revised_adaptive            93,428    13.4622        149

Read across each row of the figure to compare the original photograph, initial result,
and revised result. Several white regions become wider and more continuous, making parts
of the rims and handles easier to follow. At the same time, more of the shadows and the
dark backdrop are selected. The improvement is not the same throughout the images.

The tables show more white pixels and fewer connected regions in both revised images.
Some smaller regions may have disappeared, while others may have joined together. Those
changes also occur in the background, so the numbers alone do not show whether the mugs
were separated more accurately.

========================================================================================
STEP 5/5: Examine the results and their limits
========================================================================================

The final comparison brings both lighting directions together. Each row shows the color
photograph, the revised adaptive image, and a cyan overlay. The revised adaptive image
is the final result. The overlay simply marks those same selected pixels on the
photograph.

Look at where the cyan areas fall. Some follow the mugs' rims and handles, but others
include shadows on the tabletop and bands near the dark backdrop. The boundaries are
easier to follow in some areas, although neither photograph gives a complete separation
of both mugs.

The black mug remains difficult because its glossy surface reflects light and objects
around it. Those bright patches still belong to the mug. Meanwhile, parts of the white
mug have brightness values close to the tabletop. Even the space inside a handle
contains background that can look similar to the handle itself. The raised flowers
create additional brightness changes. A local threshold cannot tell what produced all of
these differences.

In measure_masks(), np.count_nonzero() counts the white pixels. Image % shows how much
of the full processed image they cover. Connected regions are groups of white pixels
that touch horizontally, vertically, or diagonally. These measurements help describe the
result, but they cannot tell whether the selected pixels actually belong to either mug.
Without manually labeled reference masks, segmentation accuracy cannot be calculated.

Another consideration is that the photographs have different dimensions and framing.
Resizing them to the same width does not make the scenes identical, so differences
between their measurements cannot be attributed to illumination alone. Both photographs
were also examined when choosing the revision. This shows how the settings work on these
two images; it does not establish how they will work on a new photograph.

For these photographs, the revised settings make several boundaries more continuous, but
they also include more background. That is the remaining problem with separating the
mugs using brightness alone.
